Information Architecture
Trending Eats: Designing the Discovery Architecture
Trending Eats — Personal Project · React Native, Node.js, Gemini AI
The Problem

TikTok food content is noisy and unstructured — one video at a time, no way to compare options or understand why something is trending. I designed the information architecture for Trending Eats to turn that firehose into a clear, decision-focused discovery flow for mobile users.

Key IA Decisions
  • Separate discovery from detailed information — don't front-load a restaurant's full profile
  • Surface decision-making signals quickly — trend strength, recency, and reason-to-visit
  • Use AI to organize and summarize rather than overwhelm with raw content
  • Keep the product focused on discovery — don't let it become a review aggregator
Product Flow
  • Discover a trend — surface what people are talking about right now
  • Browse restaurants — grouped by trend, not by category or location first
  • Understand why it matters — AI-summarized signal, not raw post volume
  • Decide whether to visit — one clear action at the end of the flow
IA Structure — Core Navigation:

Home (Trending Now) → ranked list of restaurant trends by signal strength
   ↳ Each card: restaurant name · why it's trending · trend strength indicator

Restaurant Detail → expanded view with AI-generated summary
   ↳ "Strong recent social activity and repeated mentions across public content."

Decision layer → single CTA: VIEW RESTAURANT →
   ↳ No noise. No competing actions. One next step.

UX Writing & AI Content
Trending Eats: UX Writing System for AI-Generated Content
Trending Eats — Personal Product Project
The Problem

The AI pipeline surfaces restaurant data from social content — but raw signal isn't useful to users. I defined the content guidelines for how the pipeline should communicate on mobile: what it says, what it omits, and how each piece of copy moves the user toward a decision.

Content Principles
  • Short. Useful. Decision-focused — every line moves the user toward a choice
  • Lead with the strongest signal, not the most recent post
  • Avoid unnecessary background — users don't need history, they need a reason to go
  • Keep AI content transparent and neutral — don't editorialize
  • Write for mobile scanning — the first 6 words carry the whole message
  • Make the next action obvious — one CTA, no competing options
Writing Decisions
  • Card headline: restaurant name only — not a tagline or descriptor
  • Trend summary: one sentence, AI-generated, written to sound like a person noticed it
  • Social signal label: "Strong recent activity" not "847 mentions this week"
  • CTA: "VIEW RESTAURANT →" — active, specific, no ambiguity
Sample card copy — as it appears in the app:

TRENDING NOW
A restaurant people are talking about

Why it's trending: Strong recent social activity and repeated mentions across public content.

VIEW RESTAURANT →

What's not there: star ratings, review counts, category tags, "as seen on TikTok" badges — all removed to reduce friction and noise at the decision point.

Workflow & Information Structure
KPMG: Restructuring an Enterprise AI Workflow
KPMG — Technology Management Consulting Intern, Jan–Apr 2026
The Context

During an enterprise transformation engagement at KPMG, I evaluated a Copilot Studio workflow being built for staffing-plan review. Scope creep had made the tool hard to define and harder to build. I identified the structural problems, refocused the solution on the core user task, and restructured the data inputs so the tool could actually function.

Workflow — Before Restructure
Raw staffing dataMultiple formats, inconsistent fields
↓
Copilot Studio intakeUnclear scope — trying to handle everything
↓
???No defined review or action step
Workflow — After Restructure
Structured spreadsheetConsistent fields: role · project · availability
↓
Copilot Studio (scoped)Surfaces gaps and conflicts only
↓
Manager review + actionApprove / flag / reassign — one row at a time
Scope Analysis — What Was In vs. Out
FeatureOriginal ScopeRecommended ScopeReason
Staffing gap detectionININCore business problem
Auto-assignment of staffINOUTIncreases complexity, removes manager judgment
Cross-project conflict flagsININHigh value, low complexity
Historical forecastingINOUTData not yet structured for it — future phase
Single structured data inputOUTINRequired for tool to function reliably
What I Identified
  • Scope creep: the workflow was trying to handle too many inputs at once, making the core user task unclear
  • Information structure issues: relevant staffing data wasn't organized in a way the tool could act on
  • Missing decision layer: there was no clear "review → action" step — the flow ended at display, not resolution
  • The core user workflow had been lost inside the feature list
What I Contributed
  • Extracted and restructured relevant staffing data into a clean spreadsheet format the tool could actually consume
  • Identified the core user workflow: Input → Organize → Review → Act
  • Refocused the solution on the business problem, not the technology capability
  • Flagged scope boundaries so the team could ship something useful rather than delay for something complete
Workflow Structure — Simplified:

INPUT: Staffing information (raw, inconsistent, multi-source)
   ↓
STRUCTURE: Relevant data organized by role, project, and availability
   ↓
REVIEW: Manager sees a clean staffing picture — gaps and conflicts surfaced
   ↓
DECISION: Approve / flag / reassign — one action per row, not a wall of data

The product lesson: Scope creep can make a useful tool harder to build and harder to use. Define the decision first, then build the information structure around it.
